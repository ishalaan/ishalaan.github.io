Design Phase

I designed Industrial Defect Intelligence as an individual computer vision project comparing classical machine learning and deep learning for steel-surface defect classification. The project used the NEU-CLS dataset, containing 1,800 images across six defect categories, with no normal or defect-free class (Cao, 2025). The objective was to determine whether a compact CNN could improve classification performance over a traditional HOG-SVM approach, while considering explainability, robustness and practical deployment requirements. An initial data audit identified one exact duplicate, leaving 1,799 images for modelling.

I used a stratified 80/20 development and test split, with five shared cross-validation folds to support a consistent comparison. The classical model combined Histogram of Oriented Gradients with Support Vector Machines, comparing linear and RBF kernels (Dalal and Triggs, 2005). The deep learning alternative was a compact PyTorch CNN containing three convolutional blocks and 6,142 trainable parameters, designed to accommodate CPU-based training. Model selection was based on development data, with the held-out test set reserved for final evaluation to reduce the risk of selection bias (Cawley and Talbot, 2010).

The evaluation framework included accuracy, precision, recall, macro F1 and confusion matrices, alongside Grad-CAM explanations, image-condition analysis and computational measurements. I also planned controlled blur and noise experiments to investigate whether improvements in clean-image accuracy remained reliable under changing conditions. These decisions reflected the need to assess not only predictive performance but also the limitations, transparency and operational suitability of the models.

Implemented Solution

I implemented the complete workflow in Python using Jupyter Notebook, scikit-learn, PyTorch and Captum. The notebook covered data preparation, five-fold cross-validation, model training, performance evaluation, explainability and robustness testing. The selected classical model was an RBF SVM with C = 10, while the CNN retained the 25-epoch training limit after a sensitivity comparison with 40 epochs. Both models were frozen before evaluation on the same 360 held-out images. The implementation also saved model files, predictions and experimental records to support reproducibility.

The CNN achieved 95.56% test accuracy and a macro F1 of 0.9555, compared with 90.28% accuracy and 0.9023 macro F1 for HOG-SVM. It made 16 classification errors rather than 35, demonstrating a clear advantage on the clean benchmark. However, the improvement was not consistent across all defect types. Pitted-surface recall fell from 85.00% with HOG-SVM to 81.67% with the CNN, highlighting the importance of examining class-level errors rather than relying on aggregate performance. Grad-CAM visualisations provided useful indications of image regions associated with CNN predictions, although these explanations could not establish causal relationships or confirm defect localisation (Selvaraju et al., 2020).

Robustness testing revealed a significant trade-off. Under controlled Gaussian blur, CNN macro F1 fell to 0.5547, while HOG-SVM retained 0.7659, reversing the clean-image ranking. Both models also performed substantially worse under noise. These findings demonstrated that higher accuracy on the original dataset did not necessarily indicate better performance under altered image conditions (Hendrycks and Dietterich, 2019). The CNN offered faster measured inference, at approximately 7.1 milliseconds compared with 29.45 milliseconds for HOG-SVM, but required substantially more training time.

The main limitations concerned the relatively small dataset, unknown production-level independence and absence of normal-steel images. Consequently, the results could not establish suitability for autonomous industrial inspection. I recommended the CNN for a supervised feasibility pilot, supported by further factory-level validation, monitoring and human oversight, rather than immediate production deployment. This approach reflects the importance of evaluating AI risks within their intended operational context (Tabassi, 2023). Overall, the project strengthened my understanding of comparative model evaluation and demonstrated that deployment decisions must consider robustness, class-specific errors and operational consequences alongside headline accuracy.

Unit 6 vs Unit 11 Evaluation

The Unit 6 Airbnb project and Unit 11 defect-classification project addressed different problems, but the first changed how I approached the second. In the group project, I concentrated on developing the modelling and analysis, comparing alternatives and making the results available to my peers. Tutor feedback helped me recognise that a technically thorough comparison still needed a clearer explanation of what the results meant for a decision. I began to question whether I was giving enough attention to interpretation alongside implementation.

This became particularly important when interpreting reviews as a proxy for demand. The Airbnb analysis could identify associations and support pricing experiments, but it could not establish how a price change would affect bookings. Shmueli's (2010) distinction between prediction and explanation helped me articulate why a useful forecast did not justify a causal recommendation. In Unit 11, I placed greater emphasis on what the evaluation could justify. Both projects used cross-validation and held-out testing. Cawley and Talbot (2010) reinforced the importance of keeping model selection separate from final evaluation. In the individual project, I also examined class-specific errors, inference time and controlled image corruption. The CNN performed better on clean images, while HOG-SVM performed better under blur. That reversal challenged the idea that selecting the highest-scoring model was enough to make a useful recommendation, consistent with Hendrycks and Dietterich's (2019) distinction between clean-image performance and corruption robustness.

Comparing the projects also broadened my understanding of bias and responsibility. In Unit 6, using reviews as a demand proxy risked underrepresenting stays that generated no review. Treating that proxy as actual demand could produce misleading recommendations for hosts. In Unit 11, the absence of normal-steel images raised a different concern about representation, while weaker pitted-surface recall showed how an overall improvement could conceal an operational weakness. I became more conscious of who would bear the consequences of an error, from hosts acting on pricing advice to inspectors relying on defect classifications. Following the risk-based approach outlined by Tabassi (2023), I would make those consequences part of evaluation from the outset. Before operational use, I would also check dataset licences, privacy implications where records identify people, and applicable safety obligations with appropriate specialists. These would be further checks, not compliance established by either project.

My thinking shifted towards explaining the conditions under which a model might be useful. I recommended a supervised feasibility pilot for Unit 11, recognising that factory-level validation and normal-steel examples were still needed. Looking across both projects, I would now define the intended decision and the evidence needed to support it earlier, then use those requirements to guide modelling and communication. The development was in my judgement as much as my technical approach.
