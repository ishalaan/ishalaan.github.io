Activity Brief

This activity explores covariance, Pearson correlation and three regression techniques through practical experimentation in Jupyter Notebook. It involves running four provided Python programmes, modifying variables and observing how changes in data points affect the strength and direction of correlations and the behaviour of regression models. The activity demonstrates how statistical relationships and predictive models respond to different datasets and variable configurations.

The completed experiments, visualisations and observations for covariance and Pearson correlation, linear regression, multiple linear regression and polynomial regression are documented in this section of the e-Portfolio, alongside a reflection on the findings and limitations of the techniques.

The activity addresses the following learning outcomes.

  • Articulate the legal, social, ethical and professional issues faced by machine learning professionals.
  • Understand the applicability and challenges associated with different datasets for the use of machine learning algorithms.
Covariance & Pearson Correlation

The covariance and Pearson correlation analysis was run with 1,000 observations and random seed 1. It generates data1 = 20 * randn(1000) + 100 and data2 = data1 + 10 * randn(1000) + 50. The original means were 100.776 and 151.050, with population standard deviations of 19.620 and 22.358. Sample covariance was calculated using NumPy and Pearson r using SciPy.

For controlled comparisons, the same data1 values and standard-normal noise vector were retained. Only the noise multiplier, direction of the relationship or scale of data2 changed.

ExperimentSample covariancePearson r

Increase noise SD to 40403.0190.4459
Use -data1 + 10 * noise + 50-380.911-0.8832
Multiply original data2 by 103897.5460.8876
Interpretation

The original scatter plot forms a clear upward band. Increasing noise broadens this band and reduces r from 0.8876 to 0.4459. Covariance nevertheless rises slightly in this finite sample: the fixed random noise vector has a small positive sample covariance with data1. Covariance alone therefore does not provide a normalised measure of association strength.

Reversing the data1 term creates a downward band and changes the sign of both statistics. Multiplying data2 by ten multiplies covariance by ten but leaves Pearson r unchanged. Covariance depends on measurement scale; Pearson r standardises covariance by the two sample standard deviations and lies between -1 and 1.

These data were constructed to have a relationship, so the findings illustrate statistical behaviour rather than evidence about a real population. Pearson r describes linear association: a value close to zero would not rule out a nonlinear relationship, and correlation alone would not establish causation.

Linear Regression

The linear regression analysis uses 13 paired observations. Its first cell imports stats but calls stat.linregress and stat.pearsonr. These calls were corrected to stats.linregress and stats.pearsonr in the executed working copy; the supplied file was preserved. No measurement units were assumed for the x and y arrays.

The original fitted model is y_hat = 103.105960 - 1.751288 * x. Pearson r is -0.7586 and training R² is 0.5755. At x = 10, the prediction is 85.5931, reproducing the reference result. This is interpolation because x = 10 lies within the observed range of 2 to 17.

Effect of changing observations

Adding 20 to every response shifts the fitted line upwards by 20 without changing its slope, correlation or R². By contrast, changing the response at the largest x value from 86 to 120 makes the fitted line almost horizontal and reduces r to -0.0742. This point has substantial leverage because its x value is far from the sample mean; changing its y value strongly influences the fit.

The scatter plots and fitted lines demonstrate why a regression summary should be examined alongside the data. An unusual observation should be investigated for measurement error or a valid explanation, rather than removed simply to improve a score. The 13 observations are too limited to establish reliable performance on new data. Residual patterns, independence and changing error variance would need examination before using inferential results or making operational predictions.

Multiple Linear Regression

The multiple regression analysis uses 36 vehicle observations, with Weight and Volume as predictors and CO2 as the response. The fitted coefficients and predictions reproduce the reference results. Weight is expressed in kg and Volume in cm³; CO2 is reported in dataset units because its measurement metadata are unspecified.

The fitted equation is CO2_hat = 79.694719 + 0.007550947 * Weight + 0.007805258 * Volume. Each coefficient describes the fitted change in CO2 for a one-unit increase in that predictor while holding the other predictor fixed. It does not establish a causal effect.

These input changes leave the fitted model unchanged. To test sensitivity to training data, the final row’s CO2 was increased from 120 to 150 and the model refitted. The Weight coefficient changed from 0.007551 to -0.003079, the Volume coefficient increased to 0.017827, and the prediction at (2300, 1300) fell to 94.2121. A coefficient sign reversal under one controlled change highlights instability; it does not demonstrate that heavier vehicles reduce emissions.

Leave-one-out validation refits the model on 35 rows and predicts the remaining row, repeating this for all 36 observations. RMSE summarises these held-out errors in CO2 dataset units; lower is better. Adding Weight improves training R² but does not improve this validation result over Volume alone. The predictors are correlated (r = 0.7535), so their individual coefficients should be interpreted cautiously. Raw coefficients also depend on units and should not be used directly to rank feature importance.

Observed weights range from 790 to 1746 kg, while engine volumes range from 900 to 2500 cm³. Both the supplied 2300 kg example and the 3300 kg variation extrapolate beyond the observed weights. These predictions demonstrate the equation rather than validated emissions estimates. The small example dataset lacks the provenance, sampling detail and additional vehicle characteristics needed for operational use.

Polynomial Regression

The polynomial regression analysis uses 18 observations of time of day and vehicle speed. The original degree-three fit was reproduced using numpy.poly1d(numpy.polyfit(x, y, 3)). Training R² is 0.943215 and the predicted speed at 17:00 is 88.8733 in the supplied speed units. Since 17 lies inside the observed interval of 1 to 22, this prediction is interpolation.

Degrees 1, 2, 3 and 5 were fitted to the same observations. For validation, each observation was held out once and the polynomial was fitted again to the other 17 points. The resulting leave-one-out RMSE measures held-out prediction error, rather than fit to the training observations.

Model complexity and sensitivity

The plotted data fall and then rise, which a straight line captures poorly. The cubic fits this pattern and has the lowest leave-one-out RMSE among the tested degrees. Degree five improves training R² only slightly while increasing held-out error, illustrating how additional flexibility can fit sample-specific variation. This small comparison favours the cubic for this dataset, but is not an independent estimate of performance after model selection.

To test sensitivity to a data point, the final observation was changed from (22, 100) to (22, 140), keeping degree three. Training R² decreased to 0.8701, while the prediction at 17 changed only slightly to 88.8018. At x = 30, however, the original cubic predicts 37.9539 and the modified cubic predicts 178.6810. These are mathematical stress tests outside the observed interval, not meaningful time-of-day forecasts. The original degree-five model even predicts -20.7985 there, demonstrating unsafe extrapolation.

R² measures improvement in squared error relative to predicting the mean; it is not the percentage of observations predicted correctly or proof of causality. R² can be negative when predictions are worse than the mean baseline. Training R² and held-out performance must be distinguished.

Reflection

The experiments distinguish linear association from predictive reliability. Increasing noise weakened Pearson correlation, while rescaling a variable changed covariance without changing correlation. Altering one observation also substantially changed the linear fit. The changed fit illustrates why a coefficient needs to be interpreted alongside the scatter plot and the data context (Schober, Boer and Schwarte, 2018).

The polynomial comparison showed why training accuracy alone is insufficient: degree five achieved a higher training R², but the cubic had lower leave-one-out error. However, choosing the cubic using those validation results means they cannot also provide an independent final performance estimate. Cawley and Talbot (2010) show how model selection itself can overfit an evaluation criterion. A stronger evaluation would separate model selection from final testing and investigate influential observations before drawing conclusions.

The vehicle analysis also demonstrates the limits of reproducibility as evidence of practical validity. Reproducing reference results does not establish that a small example dataset represents the wider vehicle population or supports reliable emissions predictions. Bender and Friedman (2018) advocate documenting dataset characteristics and limits to generalisation in language technology. For these regression analyses, that principle calls for records of provenance, units, intended use, sampling limitations and data-use permissions, alongside explicit uncertainty. This would help prevent small example datasets or extrapolated predictions from being presented as reliable evidence for wider decisions.
