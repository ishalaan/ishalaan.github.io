Activity Brief

This activity explores the societal impact of Deep Learning through its application to breast cancer detection in mammography. It examines how deep learning models analyse medical images to support cancer screening, alongside their potential benefits for diagnostic accuracy and clinical workloads. The discussion also considers ethical and socio-technical challenges, including algorithmic bias, patient privacy, fairness, accountability and human oversight.

The completed discussion forum contribution, supported by academic research and professional guidance, is documented in this section of the e-Portfolio.

The activity addresses the following learning outcomes.

  • An understanding of the motivations for, and appropriate use of, agent-based computing.
  • An understanding of the main agent models in use today and their grounding in artificial intelligence research.
Deep Learning for Breast Cancer Detection in Mammography

A deep learning application that is likely to have a significant societal impact is the use of AI to support breast cancer screening through mammography. Although mammograms are an established screening tool, identifying early signs of cancer can be difficult when suspicious features are subtle or resemble healthy breast tissue. Deep learning models can help by examining mammographic images for complex visual patterns associated with possible malignancy, providing additional support to radiologists during assessment (Wang, 2024).

How it works

Deep learning approaches used in mammography often rely on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and similar computer vision models (Wang, 2024). These systems learn from large collections of mammogram images that are paired with diagnostic labels or patient outcomes indicating whether breast cancer was identified.

During training, the neural network learns visual features associated with abnormalities such as masses, calcifications and changes in breast tissue (Wang, 2024). Unlike traditional image-processing approaches, deep learning models can learn these features directly from the data rather than requiring every relevant feature to be manually defined.

Once trained, the model can analyse a new mammogram and produce outputs such as a cancer-risk score or identify suspicious regions that may require closer examination (Wang, 2024).

Research suggests that deep learning can be effective in breast cancer screening. In a study using mammography datasets from both the UK and the USA, McKinney et al. (2020) found that an AI-based system reduced both false-positive and false-negative outcomes when compared with the original clinical assessments.

More recent evidence from Germany supports these findings. A large-scale study of over 460,000 women reported that AI-assisted mammography increased the rate at which breast cancers were detected, without leading to more women being recalled for further assessment (Eisemann et al., 2025).

Potential social and ethical impacts

The main potential benefit is earlier and more accurate detection of breast cancer. If AI helps radiologists identify cancers that might otherwise be missed, patients may be diagnosed and treated earlier. AI-supported screening may also reduce some of the workload involved in analysing large numbers of mammograms (Eisemann et al., 2025).

A key ethical issue is algorithmic bias. The performance of a deep learning model is strongly influenced by the data it is trained on. If certain populations are underrepresented in the training dataset, the model may be less accurate for those groups. Evidence also suggests that fairness observed in one medical imaging dataset may not transfer reliably when the same system is applied in a different hospital or patient population (Yang et al., 2024).

This means that high overall accuracy is not enough. Medical AI systems should also be evaluated across different demographic groups and clinical settings before they are widely deployed (Yang et al., 2024).

Another major issue is privacy. Developing deep learning systems often requires access to large collections of sensitive medical images and patient information. The World Health Organization states that AI systems used in healthcare should protect privacy, human autonomy, transparency and accountability (World Health Organization, 2021).

Another concern is accountability when AI produces an incorrect result. Missing a cancerous abnormality could postpone diagnosis and treatment, whereas incorrectly flagging a healthy mammogram may cause unnecessary distress and lead to additional scans or biopsies. The World Health Organization (2021) argues that responsibility for healthcare decisions should remain with humans, even when AI is used to support those decisions.

Another possible concern is over-reliance on automated recommendations. If clinicians trust the AI system too strongly, they may be less likely to question an incorrect result. For this reason, AI should be treated as a decision-support tool rather than as a replacement for professional clinical judgement.

Overall, I think deep learning could make breast cancer screening more effective, particularly by supporting earlier diagnosis and helping radiologists manage large screening workloads. However, its success should not be judged only by accuracy. Representative training data, clinical validation, patient privacy, fairness and continued human oversight are also essential.
