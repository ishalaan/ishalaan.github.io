Activity Brief

This activity involves developing three formative reflective case studies exploring ethical, legal and safety considerations in intelligent agent systems. It requires selecting three themes from the areas of bias in autonomous decision-making, AI safety constraints, explainable decisions, legal and ethical frameworks, and responsible deployment. Each case study examines a relevant experience, observation or design decision made during the module, critically evaluating its implications for developing reliable and responsible intelligent agents.

The three reflective case studies are documented in this section of the e-Portfolio, connecting the selected themes to practical work undertaken during the module. Each study considers what occurred, why the issue matters, the challenges and limitations identified, and how the experience informs future professional practice in intelligent agent development.

Case Study 1

Bounding Repeated Searches.

While developing the Academic Evidence Investigator, I had to decide how much freedom the Critic should have to request further searches. Re-planning could improve the evidence, but repeated requests would not necessarily resolve unavailable sources or service failures. The implementation therefore limits the search to three cycles and retains earlier evidence between attempts. This gives the agent room to revise its approach while keeping the feedback loop predictable.

Reviewing the routing logic made the boundary particularly clear. Once the limit is reached, the workflow proceeds to the Reporter even if the Critic still considers the evidence insufficient. The integration tests explicitly cover this condition. The safeguard successfully bounds repeated searching, but reaching the limit is not evidence that the research question has been answered. This distinction helped me separate operational control from output quality, especially given the hallucination risks discussed by Ji et al. (2023).

Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens (2000) frame automation as a choice about which functions to delegate and to what extent. In this project, allowing another search and accepting the resulting briefing are different decisions. Review notes and the audit trail help the researcher inspect unresolved concerns, although a completed report could still suggest more certainty than the evidence warrants.

In future work, I would make budget exhaustion a distinct completion status and test whether users recognise it as a reason to inspect the evidence. I would also assess whether the final briefing preserves the Critic's unresolved concerns. Following the behavioural testing approach discussed by Ribeiro et al. (2020), this would test the consequence of the stopping rule as well as whether the loop terminates.

Case Study 2

Making Agent Activity Understandable.

Developing the Academic Evidence Investigator raised a practical explainability question. A finished briefing showed the result, but did not make every change in the evidence-gathering process apparent. I introduced a live activity view and persistent audit log so that users could follow the investigation and revisit its recorded decisions. The interface distinguishes events such as unavailable full text, abstract fallback and further searching, helping users understand the conditions under which a report was produced.

I chose to build the activity messages from known workflow events rather than ask the language model to narrate its actions. This made the messages traceable to application behaviour. The public feed also excludes raw prompts and private diagnostic details. Functional tests check that brief stages and re-planning remain visible without exposing those details. This attention to specific behaviours reflects the testing approach advocated by Ribeiro et al. (2020).

The distinction that mattered was between visibility and understanding. A message confirming passage selection explains a workflow step, but does not establish why a particular passage supports the final conclusion. Miller (2019) emphasises the human and contextual nature of explanation. The useful question for the researcher is therefore not simply whether the system progressed, but what the recorded event means for the evidence available. Kaur et al. (2020) found that practitioners could over-trust interpretability tools, reinforcing the need to evaluate understanding rather than assume that more visible information creates it.

This experience shifted my focus towards explanations that help users act. In future, I would test whether researchers can identify abstract-only evidence, recognise service-related gaps and locate supporting passages from the report. The existing activity view and audit trail provide a strong basis for this evaluation, while user testing would establish how effectively they support informed review.

Case Study 3

Bias in Evidence Selection.

In the Academic Evidence Investigator, I used deterministic relevance rules to keep evidence selection inspectable and reduce unnecessary model calls. Title matches receive more weight than abstract matches, and domain-specific terms receive additional weight to prevent generic AI papers from dominating a focused question. These rules provide useful control over relevance, but reviewing them highlighted how my own design choices influence which research reaches the agents.

A relevant paper can describe the same idea using different terminology. For example, a question about university teaching might overlap less directly with a paper framed around tertiary pedagogy. This is a potential failure mode rather than a measured result from the project. Papers below the relevance threshold are filtered before passage selection, so semantic matching later in the workflow cannot recover a paper already excluded. This illustrates the importance of examining the full processing chain, consistent with Suresh and Guttag (2021), rather than attributing every bias to the language model.

The distinction changed how I viewed reproducibility. Applying the same rule consistently makes a decision repeatable, but does not establish that different research traditions are represented fairly. Mehrabi et al. (2021) describe multiple sources of bias across AI systems, while Baeza-Yates (2018) highlights how retrieval and ranking can shape information exposure. In this project, terminology-based exclusions could narrow the evidence and make the final briefing appear more comprehensive than it is. Retaining the broader ranked source list supports inspection but does not remove that risk.

In future, I would compare equivalent questions with different wording against a small, independently reviewed set of relevant papers. I would inspect exclusions and measure changes in coverage before adjusting thresholds or introducing synonym expansion. The aim would be to preserve the precision of the current filtering while checking that useful alternative perspectives remain discoverable.
