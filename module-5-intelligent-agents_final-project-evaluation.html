Design Phase

My contribution covered the initial team contract, the system design and the final review of the design report. The contract provided a starting point for organising the team's work, while the system design set out how a research question would move through evidence gathering, review and reporting. My priority was to give each part of the workflow a clear responsibility and make the proposed interactions understandable. Completing the final review brought these contributions together at the point of preparing the design report for submission.

The architecture separated Planner/Supervisor, Retrieval, Critic and Reporting responsibilities, drawing on the specialised agent roles discussed by Guo et al. (2024). I assigned duplicate removal and metadata cleaning to deterministic Python functions, reserving language interpretation for the LLM agents. LangGraph's shared state and conditional routing made the supervisor responsible for coordinating progress and re-planning. This favoured a controlled, inspectable workflow over unrestricted exchanges between agents. It limited their freedom to coordinate independently, but gave the design a clear route for handling evidence gaps and terminating repeated searches. The BDI mapping described the agents' knowledge, goals and plans at a conceptual level, with Archibald et al. (2024) providing a reference point for formal BDI modelling.

The Critic was designed to assess the retrieved evidence and request further searches within a fixed cycle limit. Research on self-reflection by Ji et al. (2023) provided context for including this review stage. Abstracts offered a manageable starting point for synthesis, while source links allowed the underlying publications to be consulted in greater depth. Structured JSON specified the information passed between stages, and SQLite persistence supported the planned audit trail. Together, these choices balanced agent flexibility with predictable coordination and traceability, establishing a practical specification for implementation.

Implemented Solution

The Academic Evidence Investigator develops the design into a working research-support application. It retains LangGraph coordination, the Flask interface and SQLite persistence, combining planning, retrieval, critical review and reporting. Crossref and Semantic Scholar searches produce validated, deduplicated records and structured briefings with traceable references.

The implementation extends the design by replacing Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with Qwen3-8B through Hugging Face and expanding abstract-based synthesis to PDF and HTML full-text extraction, with abstract fallback. Local sentence-transformer embeddings and FAISS select relevant passages. Article identity checks, duplicate-version handling and source locations preserve evidence traceability, while bounded retries and provider diagnostics address service interruptions. Citation normalisation and targeted grounding checks strengthen reporting.

The live activity view shows workflow progress, while the persistent audit log records decisions alongside saved reports. These additions make the stages behind a briefing visible and reviewable, improving workflow explainability, troubleshooting and human oversight. Bounded re-planning manages further searches, while passage selection and context budgets address the long-document challenges highlighted by Liu et al. (2024).

The saved final results record 263 passing Python tests across unit, integration and functional checks, including failure handling and recovery. This attention to behaviour beyond successful inputs reflects the testing principles discussed by Ribeiro et al. (2020) and provides substantial evidence of implementation quality.

Limitations include database coverage, access restrictions, API rate limits and model availability, which can restrict retrieval to abstracts. PDF extraction lacks OCR for scanned documents, and unusual or JavaScript-only layouts may fail. Passage selection and context limits can omit detail, while database, language and model biases can affect representation. The evidence gathered is therefore not exhaustive.

Hallucinations can still produce misinterpreted findings, overstated conclusions or claims unsupported by their cited sources, as discussed by Ji et al. (2023). Grounding checks and audit records support investigation but cannot guarantee accuracy, and some quality concerns appear as review notes rather than blocking completion. Ranking scores and Critic decisions are not independently validated measures of research quality. Independent assessment of retrieval relevance and claim support would complement the software tests. Hosted inference also sends research questions and selected evidence outside the application, requiring appropriate arrangements for confidential material. Source checking and human judgement remain central to effective use.
