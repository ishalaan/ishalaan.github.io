Discussion Task

This discussion explores the factors that have contributed to the rise of agent-based systems and the benefits they can offer organisations. It examines the motivations for adopting agent-based computing, including autonomy, flexibility, distributed problem-solving and coordination between intelligent agents. The activity also considers the main agent models and their foundations in artificial intelligence research.

The discussion takes place across Units 1–3, involving an initial post, peer engagement and a concluding summary. The completed contributions are documented in this section of the e-Portfolio, demonstrating academic research, critical discussion and reflection.

The activity addresses the following learning outcomes.

  • An understanding of the motivations for, and appropriate use of, agent-based computing.
  • An understanding of the main agent models in use today and their grounding in artificial intelligence research.
Initial Post
By Isaac Shalaan - Sunday, 2 August 2026, 10:14 PM

Agent-based systems have risen because organisations increasingly operate across interconnected applications, unstructured information and rapidly changing conditions. Conventional automation works well when rules and inputs are stable, but it is less suitable when software must interpret context, select tools, revise plans and pursue a broader objective. Advances in machine learning, reinforcement learning and large language models have made these capabilities more practical. Cloud services, application programming interfaces and a growing open-source ecosystem have also reduced development barriers. Frameworks such as LangChain and AutoGen provide reusable support for memory, tool use and workflow orchestration, making it easier for organisations to develop and test agent-based applications (Chowa et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2024).

The approach is grounded in several traditions of artificial intelligence. Reactive agents respond directly to environmental changes and are useful when speed and predictable behaviour matter. Deliberative agents reason about objectives and plans, while learning agents adapt through data or feedback. Hybrid agents combine rapid responses with planning or learning. These models are grounded in established AI research on planning, reinforcement learning, memory and tool use (Wang et al., 2024). Recent patterns include ReAct, which connects reasoning with tool-based action, plan-and-execute approaches that divide longer tasks into stages, and Reflexion, which uses feedback from earlier attempts (Chowa et al., 2026). The most suitable model depends on the nature of the work. Multi-agent systems may be useful where distinct agents plan, retrieve information, evaluate evidence or execute actions, although coordination creates additional cost and complexity (Chowa et al., 2026).

For organisations, the main benefit is flexible, goal-directed automation across existing systems. Agents can investigate exceptions, coordinate multi-stage processes, personalise services and refer consequential decisions to a human. Deep-research and long-horizon agents may support due diligence, regulatory intelligence and complex case analysis by gathering evidence, comparing sources and revising their approach over several steps. This extends automation beyond repetitive transactions to work where the route to an outcome cannot be fully specified in advance (Chowa et al., 2026).

However, greater autonomy does not automatically produce greater value. Long action sequences can accumulate errors, multi-agent systems may reproduce shared biases, and tool access can expose confidential data or enable unintended actions. Responsibility may also become unclear when decisions are distributed across models, developers and business owners. Stable processes may therefore remain better suited to deterministic software. Agent-based computing is most appropriate when adaptability and coordination justify the additional risks. Responsible adoption requires restricted permissions, audit trails, reliable evaluation, human approval for high-impact actions and clearly assigned accountability (Abou Ali, Dornaika and Charafeddine, 2026).

Peer Response
By Lara Koller - Thursday, 6 August 2026, 1:41 AM

Hi Isaac,

Your post provides a balanced overview of both the benefits and risks of agent-based systems. I particularly agree that greater autonomy does not necessarily produce greater value. However, I wonder whether adaptability and coordination provide a sufficiently clear basis for deciding when agent-based computing is appropriate.

Recent evidence suggests that the value of multi-agent coordination is highly task-dependent. Kim et al. (2026) found substantial performance gains for multi-agent systems in structured financial reasoning, but considerable deterioration in sequential planning tasks. They argue that coordination can become counterproductive when its complexity exceeds that of the underlying task. Similarly, Mandal et al. (2025) found that a multi-agent framework outperformed a single-agent configuration in laboratory automation. However, the LLM agents still struggled with basic tasks, sometimes deviated from instructions, and both configurations remained sensitive to minor changes in prompting. Improved relative performance therefore does not necessarily establish sufficient reliability for organisational deployment.

Tool use creates an additional challenge. Xu et al. (2025) explain that agents must determine whether a tool is needed, identify the appropriate tool and invoke it correctly across multiple steps. For simple tasks, errors in tool retrieval and parameter selection may even create more difficulty than completing the task directly.

I would therefore argue that organisations should not adopt agent-based systems primarily because a process is dynamic or requires coordination. They should first benchmark multi-agent, single-agent and deterministic alternatives using task-specific measures such as accuracy, robustness, cost and recoverability. How should organisations determine when agentic flexibility genuinely outweighs the additional coordination and reliability risks?

Dear Frederick Ogunlesi,

Thank you for your insightful post. It makes a persuasive case that economic pressure and growing technical maturity have accelerated the adoption of agent-based systems. It also identifies weak governance and unclear business value as reasons why projects may fail. Building on this point, organisations should treat governance readiness and value validation as prerequisites for scaling rather than as controls added after deployment.

A clearly defined use case is the first safeguard against poorly justified adoption. Before implementation, an organisation should establish baseline performance and specify measurable targets for accuracy, cost, completion time and required human intervention. Batool, Zowghi and Bano (2025) show that AI governance spans ethical, regulatory and operational considerations across different levels of an organisation. A staged review process could therefore require a project to demonstrate value and acceptable risk before it progresses from prototype to pilot and then to wider deployment.

Bounded autonomy would also reduce the risk of uncontrolled scaling. Agents should receive only the data, tools and permissions needed for their task, with human approval required before financial, legal, employment or customer-facing actions. Action logs, version records and rollback mechanisms would help organisations trace errors and halt unsafe behaviour. These controls are particularly important because the broader agency of agentic systems makes responsibility and accountability more difficult to allocate (Hahn, Tretter and Dabrock, 2026).

Finally, human oversight must be deliberately designed. Laux (2024) notes that overseers may lack competence or face inappropriate incentives. Named ownership, reviewer training, independent evaluation and periodic testing would make oversight more reliable. These measures directly address the risks of unclear value, weak governance and avoidable project cancellation highlighted in your post. Ultimately, organisations should only scale an agent-based pilot once its reliability, governance arrangements and organisational value have been demonstrated through controlled evaluation.

Summary Post

Throughout the units, my understanding of agent-based computing has developed from seeing agents as autonomous software capable of interacting with an environment to recognising that their value depends on how well their reasoning and architecture fit a task. I have connected the rise of agents with broader trends in intelligent computing and with the shift towards systems that can act autonomously, use tools and interact with other agents. Recent work identifies reasoning, planning, memory and tool use as central capabilities of autonomous agents (Chowa et al., 2026).

My understanding was also broadened by considering how intelligent behaviour depends on the representation of knowledge and the ability to reason over it. First-order logic demonstrated how predicates and quantifiers can express relationships formally, helping me appreciate the foundations of symbolic reasoning and the relationship between natural-language statements and machine-interpretable representations.

I have also developed a more critical view of architecture selection. Reactive agents may be suitable when rapid responses to environmental changes are required, whereas agents with state or symbolic reasoning can support decisions requiring memory or explicit reasoning. I now better understand the distinction between desires and intentions, where having possible goals differs from committing to actions towards a selected goal.

Feedback from a peer further challenged my initial assumption that adaptability and coordination were sufficient reasons to justify agentic computing. Kim et al. (2026) show that multi-agent coordination can improve some tasks while harming others, while Mandal et al. (2025) demonstrate that stronger multi-agent performance can coexist with reliability limitations.

Overall, I now see appropriate agent-based computing as an alignment between environment, reasoning requirements, architecture and organisational risk across different organisational contexts and levels of potential impact. Autonomy and complexity should therefore be justified by measurable task-specific benefits, robustness, recoverability and clear accountability, rather than treated as objectives in themselves.

Initial Post
by Isaac Shalaan - Sunday, 30 August 2026, 4:44 PM

Agent Communication Languages (ACLs) such as the Knowledge Query and Manipulation Language (KQML) address a different problem from conventional method invocation. Rather than simply specifying which operation should execute, KQML structures communication around performatives that express the intention of a message, such as requesting information, providing knowledge or asking another agent to achieve an outcome. Finin et al. (1994) describe KQML as a message format and protocol for supporting run-time knowledge sharing between agents. This is particularly useful in multi-agent systems because independently developed agents can communicate without requiring direct access to each other's internal implementation.

A major advantage of ACLs is therefore loose coupling and interoperability. An agent can communicate what it wants without prescribing exactly how another agent should accomplish it. FIPA-ACL formalised similar principles through communicative acts and message parameters including sender, receiver, content, language, ontology and conversation information (FIPA, 2002). The ontology field is particularly important because interoperability requires more than exchanging syntactically valid messages; communicating agents must also interpret domain concepts consistently. The development of FIPA-ACL was also influenced by earlier approaches such as ARCOL, which provided formally defined semantics for communicative acts (FIPA, 2005).

ACLs can therefore be valuable in distributed and heterogeneous multi-agent systems. For example, an inventory agent could request stock information from another agent without needing to know whether the receiving system obtains the answer from a database, predictive model or external service. This separation between communicative intention and internal implementation can make systems more modular and allow individual agents to evolve independently.

However, greater abstraction also introduces complexity. Agents must agree on message syntax, performative semantics, content languages and relevant ontologies. A message may therefore be structurally correct yet still be misunderstood if agents interpret its concepts differently. ACL-based interaction also requires message construction, parsing, routing, conversation management and error handling. These mechanisms introduce computational and architectural overhead compared with a direct function or method call. Security is another concern because distributed communication creates additional surfaces for authentication, authorisation and message validation. Contemporary research indicates that interoperability, scalability, latency and security remain important considerations in modern AI-agent protocols (Yang et al., 2025).

The underlying problem has therefore not disappeared with newer AI systems. ECMA-430 introduced the Natural Language Interaction Protocol (NLIP) in 2025 as an application-level protocol supporting communication between AI agents and between humans and AI agents (Ecma International, 2025). Although NLIP is not a classical speech-act ACL like KQML or FIPA-ACL, its emergence demonstrates that standardised communication between independently developed intelligent systems remains a contemporary challenge.

Method invocation in Python or Java is considerably more direct. In Python, calling a callable evaluates arguments and invokes the target object according to the language's call semantics (Python Software Foundation, 2026). Java method invocation similarly identifies an applicable method, evaluates its arguments and transfers control to the selected implementation (Oracle, 2026). This approach is efficient and predictable when components operate within a known software architecture because the caller understands the interface and expected parameters.

The disadvantage is tighter coupling. Calling object.method() normally requires knowledge of the object and its interface, whereas an ACL message represents a communicative intention and allows the receiving agent greater autonomy over how, or whether, it responds. This distinction is important because agents are expected to possess some degree of autonomy rather than behave simply as remotely invoked software components.

Overall, neither approach is inherently superior. Method invocation is appropriate for tightly integrated software where efficiency, predictable behaviour and well-defined interfaces are priorities. ACLs are better suited to distributed or heterogeneous intelligent systems where autonomy, interoperability and semantic communication are important. Their advantage therefore comes from enabling flexible agent interaction, but this flexibility introduces additional semantic, architectural, security and coordination challenges that must be justified by the requirements of the system.

Peer One Response
by Imoh Etuk - Wednesday, 2 September 2026, 5:26 AM

Isaac, your distinction between communicative intention and direct execution is persuasive, particularly your observation that an ACL allows the receiver to retain autonomy over how, or whether, it responds. Your inventory example also makes loose coupling tangible. I would, however, qualify the claim that ACLs provide interoperability. They create the conditions for interoperability but do not guarantee it. A shared message envelope can coexist with different interpretations of the performative, content or ontology. This limitation is consistent with KQML's separation of the performative from the content language and ontology (Finin et al., 1993). Payne and Tamma (2014) further show that semantic heterogeneity persists when agents possess incomplete and private knowledge of correspondences, even when both parties communicate through a structured dialogue.

Several measures could reduce this risk. First, a system could adopt a small, versioned set of performatives with explicit preconditions, expected replies and failure states. Second, message schemas and ontology identifiers should be validated at the boundary rather than trusted merely because a message parses. Third, conversation identifiers, time-outs and idempotency keys would help agents associate replies with requests and recover safely from duplicate or missing messages. Finally, authentication, authorisation and audit logs should form part of the protocol design because loose coupling can enlarge the attack surface.

Your discussion of newer protocols is useful because it shows that the interoperability problem remains current. I think the strongest architectural response is therefore layered: use ACLs between independently governed agents, but retain ordinary methods within each agent for deterministic operations. This limits semantic and messaging overhead without sacrificing autonomy where it matters. Would you make ontology-version negotiation a mandatory opening stage of every inter-agent conversation, or only when the agents have not interacted before?

Thank you for sharing the post.

The line I keep coming back to is your closing one, that the flexibility of ACLs has to be justified by the requirements of the system. I agree, and I think it raises an awkward question for the autonomy argument earlier in your post. On paper, a receiving agent can decide how, or whether, to respond. Once a system is deployed, that freedom shrinks fast. The Contract Net protocol spells out which responses are acceptable at each stage of a negotiation (Smith, 1980), and FIPA interaction protocols do much the same with their reply sequences. An agent that keeps refusing or ignoring requests has, for practical purposes, stopped participating. Wooldridge (2009) says something similar when he notes that autonomy always operates within the social conventions of the wider system.

If that holds, the overhead you describe looks harder to justify. Where protocols have already reduced interaction to fairly predictable sequences, a full ACL adds semantic and architectural cost without giving you much more than a carefully specified service interface would. My suspicion is that the real criterion is organisational. ACLs earn their keep when the agents belong to independent parties; under single ownership, they rarely do. Would you apply that test once for the whole system, or separately for each type of interaction?

Dear Aldana,

I agree with your distinction between agent communication languages and conventional method invocation, particularly your point that autonomy changes the nature of interaction. In method invocation, behaviour is determined through a predefined interface, whereas an autonomous agent retains control over whether and how it responds. Your discussion of loose coupling is also important in heterogeneous multi-agent systems, where independently developed agents may need to communicate without sharing implementation-level interfaces. Bagherzadeh and Arun-Kumar (2006), for example, demonstrate how FIPA-ACL communication can be incorporated into a flexible agent programming framework in which communicative acts and their semantics can be defined independently of conventional procedure calls.

I also agree with your concern that this flexibility introduces semantic challenges. Singh (1998) argues that agent interoperability remains problematic when communication semantics depend heavily on representations internal to individual agents, proposing greater emphasis on observable social interaction instead. This complements your point that adopting a common ACL does not necessarily guarantee that independently developed agents will interpret communication consistently.

Your observation about shared ontologies therefore remains particularly important. Even if agents recognise the same communicative structure, meaningful exchange still depends on sufficient agreement about the concepts being represented. As a useful extension, Verdicchio and Colombetti (2004) propose modelling communicative acts through social commitments between agents. This can make the consequences of communication more explicit, but it does not remove the ontology-alignment problem you identify, since agents may still conceptualise the communicated content differently.

Overall, I agree that ACLs are better suited to open, heterogeneous and autonomous environments, while their effectiveness still depends on resolving semantic and conceptual differences between participating agents.

Summary Post
by Isaac Shalaan - Tuesday, 8 September 2026, 12:03 AM

My initial position was that agent communication languages such as KQML are most valuable when autonomy, interoperability and loose coupling are more important than the efficiency and predictability of direct method invocation. KQML supports this by separating communicative intention from message content through performatives, allowing independently developed agents to interact without requiring access to each other's internal implementation (Finin et al., 1994). However, the peer responses helped me refine how far these advantages can reasonably be claimed.

One peer made an important distinction between enabling interoperability and guaranteeing it. Even when agents share a communication structure, they may interpret performatives, content or ontologies differently. This strengthened my view that syntactic compatibility alone is insufficient for genuine interoperability. Payne and Tamma (2014) demonstrate this problem through agents negotiating correspondences between incomplete and heterogeneous ontologies. I therefore now see ontology validation, versioning and negotiation as important design considerations rather than secondary implementation details.

Another peer challenged the strength of the autonomy argument. Although an ACL allows an agent to decide whether and how to respond, practical interaction protocols can constrain that freedom by defining acceptable sequences of behaviour. Smith's (1980) Contract Net Protocol illustrates how distributed agents coordinate through task announcement, bidding and award. This suggests that autonomy in multi-agent systems is not absolute, but operates within agreed interaction rules.

Together, these responses have led me to a more conditional conclusion. The choice between ACLs and method invocation should not necessarily be made once for an entire system. ACLs appear most justified at boundaries between independently developed or governed agents, where semantic negotiation and autonomy matter. Within a single controlled component, ordinary Python or Java methods may remain preferable because they avoid unnecessary messaging and semantic overhead.

My overall view is therefore that ACLs are not inherently more advanced than method invocation. Their value depends on whether the interaction genuinely requires semantic flexibility, organisational independence and negotiated coordination.

Initial Post
by Isaac Shalaan - Thursday, 1 October 2026, 12:12 AM

Generative AI systems built using deep learning present substantial ethical challenges, but I would argue that the underlying problem is not simply their ability to create convincing text or images. More fundamentally, systems such as ChatGPT and DALL-E can weaken the traditional connection between content, its source and responsibility for its consequences. This creates ethical concerns throughout the lifecycle of a deep learning system, from the data used for training to the eventual deployment of generated content.

Firstly, generative models learn statistical patterns from extremely large datasets, meaning that questions of consent, ownership and attribution arise before any output is produced. Pasetti et al. (2025) highlight continuing uncertainty surrounding the use of copyright-protected material for model training and argue that limited transparency over training datasets makes accountability particularly difficult. In my view, this creates an important ethical asymmetry: individuals may contribute creative work to an AI system's capabilities without necessarily knowing that their work was used or receiving recognition or compensation for that use.

A second concern is that generative models do not merely reproduce information; they can reproduce and amplify patterns of inequality within training data. Hagendorff (2024) identifies fairness and bias among the prominent ethical concerns surrounding generative AI, while Afreen, Mohaghegh and Doborjeh (2025) demonstrate that biased outputs can perpetuate stereotypes and disadvantage particular groups. Consequently, evaluating a model only by its technical performance is insufficient. Developers should also consider whose perspectives are represented, omitted or systematically distorted.

Perhaps the most significant issue, however, is the erosion of epistemic trust, meaning our ability to judge whether information is authentic and reliable. Generative technologies can produce plausible content without a readily verifiable human source and can enable sophisticated synthetic media. In five experiments involving 2,215 participants, Groh et al. (2024) found that political deepfakes using advanced text-to-speech audio were harder to distinguish from authentic material than equivalent deepfakes using voice-actor audio. The ethical concern therefore extends beyond individual deceptive outputs: widespread synthetic content may make authenticity itself harder to establish.

For these reasons, I do not consider generative AI inherently unethical. Its ethical acceptability depends substantially on how it is developed and deployed. Transparent data governance, bias evaluation, human oversight, content provenance mechanisms and clear accountability should therefore be treated as integral components of system design rather than safeguards introduced only after harm occurs.

Organisations using generative AI should define who is responsible when the system produces harmful or misleading content, rather than treating the AI itself as responsible. But as generative AI becomes increasingly difficult to distinguish from human-created content, who should ultimately be held responsible when existing safeguards fail-the developers, the organisations deploying these systems, or the individual who uses the generated content?

Your ScholarPlan example highlights an important ethical distinction. Making an academic claim traceable does not necessarily make it accurate. I agree that verification is essential, but would extend this to checking whether each cited passage supports the associated claim.

Gao et al. (2023) distinguish citation quality from answer correctness, demonstrating that generated answers can lack complete evidential support. This could help users identify genuine references that have been misinterpreted.

A second safeguard would be allowing the system to withhold unsupported answers. Kalai et al. (2026) show that accuracy-based evaluation can incentivise guessing rather than acknowledging uncertainty. ScholarPlan could therefore be tested using deliberately unanswerable questions, with unsupported assertions penalised more heavily than appropriate abstention. Farquhar et al. (2024) demonstrate that semantic entropy, which measures variation in meaning across generated answers, can detect some hallucinations. However, it does not address consistently incorrect outputs, so it should supplement source verification.

Human oversight also warrants evaluation. Vaccaro, Almaatouq and Malone's (2024) meta-analysis found that combinations of humans and AI, on average, underperformed the better of humans or AI alone. Although not specific to academic agents, this cautions against assuming that adding a reviewer guarantees reliability. User testing should establish whether ScholarPlan's execution trace helps researchers detect errors.

Regarding responsibility, I would argue for shared but differentiated ethical accountability. Developers should test foreseeable failures, communicate limitations and enable correction, while users should examine evidence before incorporating claims into academic work. Responsibility should reflect each party's control over the failure.

ScholarPlan's success should therefore be assessed partly by how reliably it withholds claims when supporting evidence is insufficient.

Your post identifies copyright and misinformation as important ethical concerns. I agree that oversight is necessary, but would strengthen your argument by specifying safeguards before model training and before generated content reaches audiences.

For copyright, developers should document the origins of training material and verify permissions against original sources. Longpre et al. Output testing is also important. Carlini et al. (2023) demonstrated that diffusion models can reproduce memorised training images. Their findings support testing for reproduction of training examples before deployment. Removing duplicate training items and screening outputs for close matches could form additional safeguards, although these should be evaluated rather than assumed to eliminate copying.

For deceptive content, transparency alone may provide incomplete protection. Clark and Lewandowsky (2026) found that participants could remain influenced by deepfake videos despite explicit advance warnings. Their experiments concerned judgements about individuals, so they do not establish that deepfakes changed election outcomes. Nevertheless, the findings challenge reliance on disclosure labels alone.

I would therefore recommend combining clear labels with verification of advertisers, checks on purported endorsements and rapid review of reported impersonation. Confirmed fraudulent content should be removed, with an appeals process for moderation errors. Platforms should evaluate whether these measures reduce exposure to deception, alongside monitoring incorrect removals.

These measures would translate your call for oversight into responsibilities across data collection, model evaluation and content distribution, while recognising that no single safeguard can address every ethical risk.

Summary Post
By Isaac Shalaan - Sunday, 11 October 2026, 8:57 PM

My initial post argued that generative AI is not inherently unethical, but weakens the link between content, its source and accountability for consequences. Peer feedback and module content have refined this position by highlighting the practical limits of detection and the need to assign responsibility.

One peer encouraged greater consideration of technical trade-offs in proactive provenance. Watermarking methods that influence token selection can help detect AI-generated text (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023), but must balance imperceptibility, latency and resistance to paraphrasing. Moreover, generation-time watermarking depends on providers implementing it; users of open-weight models may omit it. This limitation matters because a detectable watermark can indicate generated text, but its absence cannot establish human authorship. Provenance therefore requires more than technical safeguards.

Another peer argued that provenance labelling should accompany media literacy. I agree that literacy should prioritise source verification over identifying superficial flaws. Groh et al. (2024) found that political speech deepfakes using advanced text-to-speech were harder to distinguish than equivalent versions using voice actors. The peer also emphasised continuous bias testing, given that neural networks may reproduce biases in training data. Such testing should continue after deployment rather than being treated as a one-off assessment.

The peer further questioned accountability when safeguards fail. Data-driven learning and model opacity can complicate attributing responsibility for harmful outcomes (Santoni de Sio and Mecacci, 2021). This becomes particularly important when AI agents operate on smart shop floors or in financial services, where decisions may be rapid and lightly supervised. In high-stakes settings, interpretable models may be preferable to black-box alternatives (Rudin, 2019).

I believe primary responsibility should rest with developers and deploying organisations, who typically exercise greater control over system design and operation than end users. As a practitioner, I would treat accountability as a fundamental ethical design requirement assessed alongside accuracy and efficiency.
