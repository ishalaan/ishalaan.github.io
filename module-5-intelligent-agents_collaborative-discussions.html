Discussion Task

This discussion explores the factors that have contributed to the rise of agent-based systems and the benefits they can offer organisations. It examines the motivations for adopting agent-based computing, including autonomy, flexibility, distributed problem-solving and coordination between intelligent agents. The activity also considers the main agent models and their foundations in artificial intelligence research.

The discussion takes place across Units 1–3, involving an initial post, peer engagement and a concluding summary. The completed contributions are documented in this section of the e-Portfolio, demonstrating academic research, critical discussion and reflection.

The activity addresses the following learning outcomes.

  • An understanding of the motivations for, and appropriate use of, agent-based computing.
  • An understanding of the main agent models in use today and their grounding in artificial intelligence research.
Initial Post

by Isaac Shalaan - Sunday, 2 August 2026, 10:14 PM

Agent-based systems have risen because organisations increasingly operate across interconnected applications, unstructured information and rapidly changing conditions. Conventional automation works well when rules and inputs are stable, but it is less suitable when software must interpret context, select tools, revise plans and pursue a broader objective. Advances in machine learning, reinforcement learning and large language models have made these capabilities more practical. Cloud services, application programming interfaces and a growing open-source ecosystem have also reduced development barriers. Frameworks such as LangChain and AutoGen provide reusable support for memory, tool use and workflow orchestration, making it easier for organisations to develop and test agent-based applications (Chowa et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2024).

The approach is grounded in several traditions of artificial intelligence. Reactive agents respond directly to environmental changes and are useful when speed and predictable behaviour matter. Deliberative agents reason about objectives and plans, while learning agents adapt through data or feedback. Hybrid agents combine rapid responses with planning or learning. These models are grounded in established AI research on planning, reinforcement learning, memory and tool use (Wang et al., 2024). Recent patterns include ReAct, which connects reasoning with tool-based action, plan-and-execute approaches that divide longer tasks into stages, and Reflexion, which uses feedback from earlier attempts (Chowa et al., 2026). The most suitable model depends on the nature of the work. Multi-agent systems may be useful where distinct agents plan, retrieve information, evaluate evidence or execute actions, although coordination creates additional cost and complexity (Chowa et al., 2026).

For organisations, the main benefit is flexible, goal-directed automation across existing systems. Agents can investigate exceptions, coordinate multi-stage processes, personalise services and refer consequential decisions to a human. Deep-research and long-horizon agents may support due diligence, regulatory intelligence and complex case analysis by gathering evidence, comparing sources and revising their approach over several steps. This extends automation beyond repetitive transactions to work where the route to an outcome cannot be fully specified in advance (Chowa et al., 2026).

However, greater autonomy does not automatically produce greater value. Long action sequences can accumulate errors, multi-agent systems may reproduce shared biases, and tool access can expose confidential data or enable unintended actions. Responsibility may also become unclear when decisions are distributed across models, developers and business owners. Stable processes may therefore remain better suited to deterministic software. Agent-based computing is most appropriate when adaptability and coordination justify the additional risks. Responsible adoption requires restricted permissions, audit trails, reliable evaluation, human approval for high-impact actions and clearly assigned accountability (Abou Ali, Dornaika and Charafeddine, 2026).

Peer Response

by Lara Koller - Thursday, 6 August 2026, 1:41 AM

Hi Isaac,

Your post provides a balanced overview of both the benefits and risks of agent-based systems. I particularly agree that greater autonomy does not necessarily produce greater value. However, I wonder whether adaptability and coordination provide a sufficiently clear basis for deciding when agent-based computing is appropriate.

Recent evidence suggests that the value of multi-agent coordination is highly task-dependent. Kim et al. (2026) found substantial performance gains for multi-agent systems in structured financial reasoning, but considerable deterioration in sequential planning tasks. They argue that coordination can become counterproductive when its complexity exceeds that of the underlying task. Similarly, Mandal et al. (2025) found that a multi-agent framework outperformed a single-agent configuration in laboratory automation. However, the LLM agents still struggled with basic tasks, sometimes deviated from instructions, and both configurations remained sensitive to minor changes in prompting. Improved relative performance therefore does not necessarily establish sufficient reliability for organisational deployment.

Tool use creates an additional challenge. Xu et al. (2025) explain that agents must determine whether a tool is needed, identify the appropriate tool and invoke it correctly across multiple steps. For simple tasks, errors in tool retrieval and parameter selection may even create more difficulty than completing the task directly.

I would therefore argue that organisations should not adopt agent-based systems primarily because a process is dynamic or requires coordination. They should first benchmark multi-agent, single-agent and deterministic alternatives using task-specific measures such as accuracy, robustness, cost and recoverability. How should organisations determine when agentic flexibility genuinely outweighs the additional coordination and reliability risks?

Kim, Y. et al. (2026) ‘Capable language models can outgrow the benefits of collaboration’, Nature Machine Intelligence, 8, pp. 1157-1172. Available at: https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-026-01268-y

Reply

by Isaac Shalaan - Friday, 14 August 2026, 7:32 PM

Hi Lara,

Your point that adaptability and coordination are not sufficient criteria for adopting agent-based systems has made me reconsider how I framed this in my initial post. I argued that agentic computing is appropriate when these benefits justify the additional risks, but this leaves unanswered how an organisation should establish that justification. I now think comparison with simpler architectures should be part of the decision itself.

Kim et al. (2026) provide strong support for this approach. Their results show that multi-agent coordination can produce substantial gains in some tasks while significantly reducing performance in others. This suggests that architectural complexity should demonstrate task-specific value rather than being assumed to benefit complex work.

I also found your point that improved relative performance does not necessarily establish sufficient reliability for organisational deployment particularly useful. Mandal et al. (2025) found that their multi-agent configuration outperformed single-agent tool integration with GPT-4o, yet the agents still struggled with basic tasks, deviated from instructions and remained sensitive to minor changes in prompting. This has shifted my thinking from asking whether agents can improve a process to whether that improvement is sufficiently reliable for operational use.

I would therefore build on your proposed benchmarking by considering the consequences of failure alongside accuracy, robustness, cost and recoverability. Organisations should also consider whether errors can be detected, reversed or safely escalated to humans. Agentic flexibility may be worthwhile where measurable improvements outweigh these risks, whereas deterministic or single-agent approaches remain preferable when additional coordination does not demonstrate sufficient value.

Summary Post

by Isaac Shalaan - Friday, 14 August 2026, 8:14 PM

Throughout the units, my understanding of agent-based computing has developed from seeing agents as autonomous software capable of interacting with an environment to recognising that their value depends on how well their reasoning and architecture fit a task. I have connected the rise of agents with broader trends in intelligent computing and with the shift towards systems that can act autonomously, use tools and interact with other agents. Recent work identifies reasoning, planning, memory and tool use as central capabilities of autonomous agents (Chowa et al., 2026).

My understanding was also broadened by considering how intelligent behaviour depends on the representation of knowledge and the ability to reason over it. First-order logic demonstrated how predicates and quantifiers can express relationships formally, helping me appreciate the foundations of symbolic reasoning and the relationship between natural-language statements and machine-interpretable representations.

I have also developed a more critical view of architecture selection. Reactive agents may be suitable when rapid responses to environmental changes are required, whereas agents with state or symbolic reasoning can support decisions requiring memory or explicit reasoning. I now better understand the distinction between desires and intentions, where having possible goals differs from committing to actions towards a selected goal.

Feedback from a peer further challenged my initial assumption that adaptability and coordination were sufficient reasons to justify agentic computing. Kim et al. (2026) show that multi-agent coordination can improve some tasks while harming others, while Mandal et al. (2025) demonstrate that stronger multi-agent performance can coexist with reliability limitations.

Overall, I now see appropriate agent-based computing as an alignment between environment, reasoning requirements, architecture and organisational risk across different organisational contexts and levels of potential impact. Autonomy and complexity should therefore be justified by measurable task-specific benefits, robustness, recoverability and clear accountability, rather than treated as objectives in themselves.
