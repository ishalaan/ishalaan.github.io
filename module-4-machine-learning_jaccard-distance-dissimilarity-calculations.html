Activity Brief

This activity explores Jaccard distance and dissimilarity as measures for comparing individuals using categorical pathological test results. It involves examining the recorded gender, fever, cough and four test outcomes for Jack, Mary and Jim, and calculating the Jaccard distance or dissimilarity for each of the three specified pairs.

The supplied dataset, pairwise calculations and explanations of the results are documented in this section of the e-Portfolio, followed by a reflection on the suitability and limitations of applying Jaccard measures to the data.

The activity addresses the following learning outcomes.

  • Articulate the legal, social, ethical and professional issues faced by machine learning professionals.
  • Understand the applicability and challenges associated with different datasets for the use of machine learning algorithms.
Test Results

To calculate the Jaccard coefficient, we first convert the asymmetric variables to binary values and re-write the table. Since Gender is a symmetric variable (that is, male, female have the same weight), it is not converted.

So let Y & P = 1; N & A = 0

Jaccard=f01+f10f01+f10+f11

Binary Conversion

For the six asymmetric attributes, 1 indicates Y or P, and 0 indicates N or A. Applying the specified conversion gives the following table.

In the formula, f01 counts attributes where the first person has 0 and the second has 1. The value f10 counts the reverse, while f11 counts attributes where both have 1. Shared zeros, represented by f00, are excluded because the Jaccard measure is intended to compare the presence of the asymmetric attributes.

Jaccard Calculations

The three pairwise dissimilarities are calculated using the six binary attributes in the preceding tab. For each pair, attributes with different values contribute to the numerator, while attributes where both values are 1 also contribute to the denominator. Attributes where both values are 0 do not contribute to either.

Jack and Mary

Jack and Mary both have a value of 1 for Fever and Test-1, giving f11 = 2. They differ on Test-3, where Jack has 0 and Mary has 1, giving f01 = 1 and f10 = 0. Cough, Test-2 and Test-4 are shared zeros, giving f00 = 3.

Jaccard(Jack, Mary) = (1 + 0) / (1 + 0 + 2) = 1 / 3 ≈ 0.33

Jack and Jim

Jack and Jim both have a value of 1 for Fever, giving f11 = 1. They differ on Cough, where Jack has 0 and Jim has 1, and on Test-1, where Jack has 1 and Jim has 0. Therefore, f01 = 1 and f10 = 1. Test-2, Test-3 and Test-4 are shared zeros, giving f00 = 3.

Jaccard(Jack, Jim) = (1 + 1) / (1 + 1 + 1) = 2 / 3 ≈ 0.67

Jim and Mary

Jim and Mary both have a value of 1 for Fever, giving f11 = 1. They differ on Cough, where Jim has 1 and Mary has 0, and on Test-1 and Test-3, where Jim has 0 and Mary has 1. Therefore, f10 = 1 and f01 = 2. Test-2 and Test-4 are shared zeros, giving f00 = 2.

Jaccard(Jim, Mary) = (2 + 1) / (2 + 1 + 1) = 3 / 4 = 0.75

The calculations reproduce all three expected results. Jack and Mary have the lowest dissimilarity, while Jim and Mary have the highest. A lower Jaccard distance indicates greater similarity in the presence of the recorded binary attributes.

Reflection

Completing this activity helped me understand that the choice of how categorical data are represented has a direct effect on the meaning of a similarity or dissimilarity measure. Converting the fever, cough and test results into binary indicators made the three comparisons possible, but the important step was distinguishing attributes shared as present from attributes shared as absent. I found that calculating the individual counts before applying the formula made it easier to check the working and avoid treating matching zeros as evidence of similarity.

The results show that Jack and Mary are the most similar pair in terms of the recorded positive indicators, with a Jaccard dissimilarity of 0.33. Jack and Jim have a higher distance of 0.67, while Jim and Mary are the most dissimilar at 0.75. These results illustrate how the measure focuses on mismatches relative to the attributes present in at least one individual. They do not establish whether the individuals have the same medical condition, because the calculation compares the encoded records rather than their clinical significance.

A limitation is that the conversion treats N and A as equivalent zeros, although those original labels may represent different meanings. The method also gives equal importance to Fever, Cough and each test, even though the relevance of those indicators may differ in practice. Excluding Gender is consistent with the specified asymmetric-binary calculation, but it also demonstrates that the selection of attributes changes what is being compared. With only three records, the activity illustrates the calculation rather than providing evidence for general conclusions about patient groups.

In a real machine-learning application involving pathological results, I would first confirm the meaning of every category, establish whether missing or unavailable values should be handled separately and assess whether equal feature weighting is appropriate. Medical information also requires careful protection of privacy and appropriate access controls. Overall, the activity reinforced the importance of transparent data preparation, checking each calculation and interpreting numerical outputs within the context and limitations of the underlying dataset.
