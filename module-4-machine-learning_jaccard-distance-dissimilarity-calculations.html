Activity Brief

This activity explores Jaccard distance and dissimilarity as measures for comparing individuals using categorical pathological test results. It involves examining the recorded gender, fever, cough and four test outcomes for Jack, Mary and Jim, and calculating the Jaccard distance or dissimilarity for each of the three specified pairs.

The supplied dataset, pairwise calculations and explanations of the results are documented in this section of the e-Portfolio, followed by a reflection on the suitability and limitations of applying Jaccard measures to the data.

The activity addresses the following learning outcomes.

  • Articulate the legal, social, ethical and professional issues faced by machine learning professionals.
  • Understand the applicability and challenges associated with different datasets for the use of machine learning algorithms.
Test Results

The comparison uses six binary indicators: Fever, Cough and Test-1 to Test-4. The question is how much the recorded positive indicators differ between each pair of individuals. Gender is retained in the source table for completeness but excluded from this calculation; it is a nominal category, not an indicator of a symptom or positive test.

Distance and Similarity

Jaccard distance measures mismatches as a proportion of attributes present in at least one record: d_J = (f_01 + f_10) / (f_11 + f_01 + f_10). Jaccard similarity is its complement: J = f_11 / (f_11 + f_01 + f_10) = 1 - d_J. These are distinct quantities: lower distance means greater similarity.

For an ordered pair, f_01 counts attributes present only in the second record, f_10 counts those present only in the first, and f_11 counts shared presences. Shared zeros, f_00, are excluded. “Asymmetric binary” refers to this unequal treatment of presence and absence; the distance itself is symmetric, so reversing the two individuals does not change the result.

Jaccard Calculations

Each comparison uses the six encoded indicators with equal weight. The numerator counts mismatches and the denominator counts the union of positive indicators. The counts below were checked against the encoded records and independently verified using SciPy’s Jaccard distance implementation.

Jack and Mary

Fever and Test-1 are shared positives, giving f_11 = 2. Only Mary is positive for Test-3, so f_01 = 1 and f_10 = 0. Cough, Test-2 and Test-4 are shared zeros, giving f_00 = 3.

d_J(Jack, Mary) = (1 + 0) / (2 + 1 + 0) = 1/3 ≈ 0.33. The corresponding similarity is 2/3 ≈ 0.67.

Jack and Jim

Fever is the only shared positive, giving f_11 = 1. Jim alone is positive for Cough and Jack alone is positive for Test-1, so f_01 = 1 and f_10 = 1. Test-2, Test-3 and Test-4 are shared zeros, giving f_00 = 3.

d_J(Jack, Jim) = (1 + 1) / (1 + 1 + 1) = 2/3 ≈ 0.67. The corresponding similarity is 1/3 ≈ 0.33.

Jim and Mary

Fever is the only shared positive, giving f_11 = 1. Mary alone is positive for Test-1 and Test-3, while Jim alone is positive for Cough. Thus f_01 = 2, f_10 = 1 and f_00 = 2, with Test-2 and Test-4 excluded as shared zeros.

d_J(Jim, Mary) = (2 + 1) / (1 + 2 + 1) = 3/4 = 0.75. The corresponding similarity is 1/4 = 0.25.

Jack and Mary have the lowest distance and Jim and Mary the highest. These values describe differences among the positive indicators in each pair’s union; they are not the proportion of all six attributes that differ, nor probabilities of different diagnoses. The fractions are retained to distinguish exact results from two-decimal rounding.

Test-2 and Test-4 are zero for all three individuals, so removing those two columns would leave every Jaccard distance unchanged. Fever is positive for everyone and contributes a shared presence to every denominator. The result therefore depends on which indicators are included, even when an indicator is constant across these records.

Reflection

The calculations show that a distance is meaningful only in relation to its encoding and feature selection. Excluding shared zeros focuses the comparison on recorded presences, while the distinction between similarity and distance prevents the ranking from being reversed. Jaccard distance is a metric on sets, including the triangle inequality (Kosub, 2019), but this mathematical property does not establish clinical relevance. The values compare encoded records, not diagnoses or disease severity.

The most consequential assumption is the mapping of both N and A to zero. If those labels distinguish a negative result from an unavailable result, merging them would discard information. Equal weighting also treats every symptom and test as equally relevant. If nominal or other variable types were included, a measure designed for mixed data, such as Gower’s coefficient, would provide a more appropriate starting point than adding arbitrary category codes to the binary vectors (Gower, 1971).

With three records, the analysis demonstrates the method rather than supporting conclusions about a wider population. Bender and Friedman (2018) link dataset documentation to clearer limits on generalisation in language technology; the same documentation principle is useful here for category definitions, sampling and intended use. Any application to identifiable health records would also require appropriate data-use permissions, privacy safeguards and justified feature selection. A low distance alone would not support a clinical decision.
