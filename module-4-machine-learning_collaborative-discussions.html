Discussion Task

This discussion explores the impact of Industry 4.0 and Industry 5.0 on organisations and the wider business environment. It examines the role of emerging technologies, alongside the consequences of information system failures for customers, operational performance, financial costs and organisational reputation. The discussion considers the opportunities and challenges associated with technological transformation and the importance of reliable information systems.

The discussion takes place across Units 1–3, involving an initial post and engagement with relevant academic literature. The completed contributions are documented in this section of the e-Portfolio, demonstrating research, critical analysis and reflection.

The activity addresses the following learning outcomes.

  • Understand the applicability and challenges associated with different datasets for the use of machine learning algorithms.
  • Systematically develop and implement the skills required to be an effective member of a development team in a virtual professional environment, adopting real-life perspectives on team roles and organisation.
Initial Post
by Isaac Shalaan - Monday, 3 August 2026, 8:55 PM

In my role developing sales reporting, price-optimisation and revenue-forecasting systems for the UK automotive aftermarket, Industry 4.0 is evident in interconnected databases, cloud platforms and machine-learning pipelines. These technologies combine sales, stock and market data to support pricing and replenishment. Peer-reviewed research identifies intermittent demand as a central difficulty in spare-parts forecasting (Pinçe, Turrini and Meissner, 2021). Using automotive-aftermarket data, Zhuang, Yu and Chen (2022) found that combining demand-occurrence classification with quantity forecasting can improve accuracy and support inventory decisions. Industry 5.0 nevertheless requires efficiency to be balanced with human-centricity, sustainability and resilience (Metcalf, 2024).

The CDK Global incident demonstrates the risks created by this interconnectivity and dependence. CDK Global is a third-party automotive technology provider whose software-as-a-service dealer-management platform supports customer relationships, sales, financing, servicing, inventory and back-office operations (Group 1 Automotive, Inc., 2024). On 19 June 2024, CDK suspended affected systems following a cybersecurity incident (Sonic Automotive, Inc., 2024). Reuters reported that the platform served more than 15,000 retail locations and that some dealerships reverted to manual paperwork (Deka and Eckert, 2024). CDK began restoring access on 26 June 2024 (Sonic Automotive, Inc., 2024).

AutoNation and Sonic Automotive were dealership groups reliant on CDK. Sonic reported that the outage impaired vehicle deliveries and prevented transactions from being completed within normal timeframes. It estimated a reduction of approximately $30 million in second-quarter pre-tax income (Sonic Automotive, Inc., 2024). AutoNation regained its core functions on 29 June 2024, although ordering, scheduling, payment and reporting integrations were not fully restored until the end of July 2024. It attributed an estimated 5% reduction in quarterly gross profit to the outage and paid approximately $43 million in one-off employee compensation to maintain continuity (AutoNation, Inc., 2024). Therefore, customer-facing delays could damage dealership reputations because customers experienced the supplier's failure through the retailer.

For aftermarket analytics, the outage could create missing transactions, delayed back-entry, duplicates and abnormal zero-sales periods. Exploratory data analysis should identify these discontinuities before models are retrained. Otherwise, correlations between price and volume may be distorted, while regression models may misinterpret system unavailability as reduced demand or greater price sensitivity. Forecasting errors could then propagate into pricing, inventory and revenue decisions.

Critically, the incident supports Metcalf's (2024) warning that efficiency optimisation can reduce resilience when interconnected systems lack effective alternatives. Manual processes allowed dealerships to remain open, but with lower productivity (Deka and Eckert, 2024). Abandoning automation would therefore not resolve the problem. An Industry 5.0 response should combine digital efficiency with offline transaction capture, tested failover procedures, supplier-risk controls, auditable data provenance and explicit outage indicators in modelling datasets. Although the reported losses concern individual dealership groups and cannot be generalised across the sector, they show that system availability and data integrity are prerequisites for reliable machine-learning forecasts. Even an accurate model cannot support pricing or revenue decisions when its input data is missing, delayed or distorted.

Thank you for this interesting and well-researched case.

The reliance on a single, well-established vendor and its software solutions has revealed weaknesses in the system resilience layer, while also underscoring an emphasis on efficiency-driven gains across all parties.

For automotive clients, protective measures should focus on what is within their control: ensuring timely business recovery and continuity through robust backup plans and disaster recovery solutions, following the NIST incident response framework (Nelson et al., 2025). Reliable manual workflows, extensively tested procedures, and clear business rules for critical business processes can guarantee operational resilience and business continuity. Importantly, the objective is not just a successful backup, but the effective restoration of trustworthy, reliable data. A comprehensive reconciliation plan-aligning manual entries with system data across sales, inventory, customer records, financial transactions, and forecasting-supported by validation procedures and quality checks, helps ensure data integrity. This step is critical before resuming operations and increasing reliance on algorithmic analysis. As Campbell (2026) states, even a small data inaccuracy can be amplified by Artificial Intelligence (AI), potentially leading to significant business harm. Furthermore, continuous, in-depth monitoring across all organisational layers is essential for post-recovery, anomaly detection and for preventing inaccurate AI-based predictions. This process helps restore trust in data, supports informed decision-making, and reinforces the organisation's credibility. As Meclaf (2024) emphasises, trust is foundational to our society and is upheld by responsible governance, resilience, transparency and human oversight, allowing for human-centred wellbeing while maintaining confidence in underlying technology.

I really enjoyed your post because it addresses a very concrete case in an industry that I am particularly interested in. The financial and reputational risks are clear, but I particularly appreciated how you connected the disruption to its impact on the analytical system itself. I also found Zhuang, Yu, and Chen (2022) particularly interesting, as I have encountered similar scenarios involving missing or irregular data in retail. Their distinction between intermittent, lumpy ,and smooth demand highlights how different data patterns require appropriate modeling strategies.

This also made me think about resilience and maintainability in Industry 5.0. Compare and Zio (2025) emphasise the importance of combining AI with reliability and maintenance engineering, human expertise ,and continuous learning. More recently, predictive-maintenance research suggests that resilience can also be proactive: anomaly detection can identify deviations from normal operating patterns before they develop into failures, while generative models can help address the scarcity of failure data (Rajput et al., 2026).

However, I think resilience also depends on how AI-enabled systems are architected in the first place. AI can generate technically plausible code, but it may not fully understand the broader architectural context, domain constraints or long-term trade-offs. Bucaioni et al. (2026) identify context-aware reasoning and domain-specific expertise as persistent challenges for AI-supported software architecture. This makes architectural knowledge an important validation layer in AI-assisted development: for example, decisions around idempotency, asynchronous queues or even whether to use technologies such as blockchain can have significant implications for resilience, scalability and data integrity that may not be captured by the generated code alone.

Applied to the CDK case, this suggests that resilience should extend beyond recovering the system after an outage to protecting the data, architecture and AI lifecycle itself. Anomaly detection could identify abnormal transaction patterns following an incident, while data validation and provenance checks could prevent corrupted or incomplete data from entering forecasting models. At the architectural level, appropriate mechanisms such as idempotent processing and asynchronous communication could further reduce the risk of failures propagating across dependent services. This is particularly important because models developed on controlled datasets may not necessarily remain reliable under real-world conditions involving noise, missing data and changing environments (Rajput et al., 2026).

Overall, I think your example demonstrates that Industry 5.0 should not only aim for digital efficiency, but for systems that are resilient by design, maintainable and capable of detecting and managing data degradation before it propagates into business decisions.

by John-Jon Steyn - Monday, 17 August 2026, 5:07 PM

Hey Isaac,

Your post identifies an important consequence of the CDK outage: system recovery and data recovery are different events. I agree that the damage extends beyond interrupted sales. An unavailable dealer-management system changes what the dataset means. Under normal operation, zero recorded sales may represent no demand; during an outage, the same value may mean that demand was not observed. If those cases are combined, occurrence forecasts, price-elasticity estimates and replenishment decisions may all be biased.

I would extend your proposal by treating outage handling as a formal data-governance process. Records could include transaction time, entry time, source-system status, a back-entry indicator and reconciliation status. Before retraining, delayed entries should be allocated to their actual transaction dates where evidence permits, duplicates removed, and affected periods compared with finance and inventory records. This would convert auditable provenance into a practical control.

The supplier-risk issue also deserves a dependency-level view. An alternative application offers limited protection if both systems depend on the same identity service, hosting platform or integration layer. NIST's supply-chain guidance expects organisations to prepare continuity arrangements for externally supplied services so interruptions can be contained and operations restored promptly (Boyens et al., 2022).

AutoNation's experience reinforces your argument: central dealer-system access returned on 29 June, yet supporting transactional and reporting connections remained incomplete until late July (AutoNation, 2024). I would therefore measure resilience at three points: restored transaction processing, restored integrations, and data confirmed suitable for model use. This preserves the efficiency of connected analytics while ensuring that human reviewers can distinguish genuine demand from impaired observability.

Hi Rafaela,

Your post links counterfeit safety parts to two related weaknesses: fragmented supply-chain records and the limitations of image-based detection when data is incomplete or inconsistent. This suggests that computer vision should be one control within a wider traceability system rather than the only preventative measure. Safety-critical components could be assigned unique identifiers linked to interoperable records covering manufacture, distribution, sale and installation, thereby supporting authentication, targeted recalls and accountability. Ahmed and MacCarthy (2023) show that traceability effectiveness depends on both how many supply-chain stages are covered and how much information is recorded at each stage.

Blockchain is relevant only as one possible way to implement that shared record. Alsadi et al. (2023) demonstrate blockchain-based certification across multiple tiers of an automotive supply chain, although their study does not concern counterfeit aftermarket parts directly. Moreover, an immutable record cannot establish authenticity if inaccurate data are entered initially. Supplier accreditation, digitally signed records, audits and procedures for revoking compromised identifiers would therefore remain essential. Zhang, Zhang and Feng (2025) compare blockchain adoption with traditional anti-counterfeiting measures, suggesting that layered controls are preferable to reliance on one technology.

To address the image variability and limited labelled data you identify, training and validation sets should include different angles, lighting conditions, packaging states and damaged parts. Low-confidence cases should be referred to trained inspectors, while false-negative rates and model drift should be monitored because approving a counterfeit airbag or brake component creates far greater harm than delaying a genuine part.

Finally, customs authorities and online marketplaces could share intelligence, verify sellers and prioritise the inspection of small consignments. The OECD and European Union Intellectual Property Office (2025) identify small parcels, online platforms and localisation as persistent enforcement challenges. Overall, prevention requires interoperable traceability, reliable source data, AI-assisted inspection and human oversight.

Your post shows how a vulnerability in one widely used third-party product created cascading effects across many organisations. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) and the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) (2023) reported that the CL0P group exploited CVE-2023-34362, a previously unknown SQL-injection vulnerability in MOVEit Transfer, in May 2023. Prevention must therefore address both the likelihood of vulnerable software reaching production and the scale of harm following exploitation.

At the developer level, Progress Software could have strengthened its secure software-development lifecycle through threat modelling, parameterised database queries, code review, static analysis, dynamic application testing and independent penetration testing. Mohammed et al. (2017) identify static and dynamic analysis as established approaches for integrating security checks into software development. These measures cannot guarantee that every zero-day flaw will be found, but they could increase the likelihood of detecting an SQL-injection weakness before release.

Organisations using MOVEit could also have limited their exposure by treating trusted third-party software as potentially compromisable. Internet-facing MOVEit servers could have been segmented from unnecessary internal systems, with service and database accounts restricted through least privilege. Data-retention periods should have been minimised, while abnormal database access, download volumes and transfer destinations should have triggered alerts. Hammi and Zeadally (2023) support a holistic, end-to-end approach to software supply-chain security. Such controls might not have prevented the initial exploit, but they could have reduced the volume of accessible data and limited the wider consequences.

Finally, organisations should maintain inventories of internet-facing third-party applications and rehearse rapid isolation, investigation and patching. A resilient Industry 5.0 response requires secure-by-design software, customer-side containment and tested incident-response arrangements.

Initial Post
by Isaac Shalaan - Tuesday, 22 September 2026, 8:23 PM

The strongest argument for using AI writers, in my view, is the time they can release for work that requires human judgement. However, producing text quickly is only useful if that text serves its intended purpose. Hutson (2021) highlights the gap between fluent language generation and understanding. This raises a practical concern across writing tasks. Readers may find a polished response convincing even when its content has not been verified.

In administrative work, I see considerable value in using AI to prepare routine correspondence and initial report drafts. Noy and Zhang (2023) found that ChatGPT reduced average completion time by 40% and increased assessed quality by 18% in an experiment involving 453 professionals. These results provide evidence of benefits for the tasks studied. Nevertheless, I would be cautious about interpreting them as proof that an organisation should automate its writing processes more widely. A draft email confirming an appointment and a letter explaining an employment decision may look similar as writing tasks, but the consequences of an error differ substantially. I would therefore assess whether time savings remain after checking facts, correcting mistakes and reviewing the appropriateness of the wording.

Academic and professional writing make this distinction particularly important. Walters and Wilder (2023) found that 18% of references generated by GPT-4 in their study were fabricated. This was a result for a particular model under specific conditions, rather than an error rate that applies to every AI writer. Even so, it illustrates why a credible appearance cannot substitute for evidence. My position is that AI can assist with expression and structure, while the writer must verify the underlying claims and consult the original sources. Otherwise, a clearer presentation may simply make an unsupported argument more persuasive.

Creative writing requires a different assessment because factual accuracy is not always its main purpose. Doshi and Hauser (2024) found that AI-generated ideas improved evaluations of individual short stories, particularly among less creative participants, while increasing similarity between stories. What interests me here is that an individual benefit can coexist with a collective disadvantage. A writer may receive useful inspiration, yet widespread reliance on similar suggestions could reduce the variety of stories produced. However, the experiment involved short stories and non-professional participants, which limits conclusions about professional novelists.

I would therefore support selective use of AI writers, with oversight determined by the task. Administrative drafts need checks against reliable information. Academic writing requires independent source verification. Creative work needs deliberate human choices about voice and direction. Across these settings, I would also prioritise confidentiality and review potentially biased wording. For me, successful adoption means demonstrating that the finished work is useful and trustworthy, with a person clearly responsible for its content.

Your final point about humans remaining capable of questioning and correcting AI is particularly important. I would therefore see human oversight, clearer system limitations and stronger dataset documentation as complementary safeguards. Clear disclosure of AI involvement in written material could further strengthen accountability by making the role of AI more transparent while still allowing it to support legitimate administrative and creative tasks.

Summary Post
by Isaac Shalaan - Saturday, 3 October 2026, 2:16 PM

My initial post supported selective use of AI writers, provided that efficiency did not compromise reliability. The units and peer feedback have refined this position by making the requirements for effective oversight more explicit.

Studying neural network training helped me distinguish reducing training loss from eliminating factual errors. Backpropagation provides gradients used to adjust network weights. Successful optimisation does not guarantee truthfulness. Exploring Transformers clarified the architecture behind GPT, whose autoregressive training involves predicting subsequent tokens (Brown et al., 2020). This connects with Hutson's (2021) discussion of fluent language generation without understanding.

A peer reinforced my argument that administrative benefits should be assessed using total time, including verification and correction. Their suggestion to test responses against approved policies, including after policy changes, connects with the units' coverage of monitoring within MLOps. Uncertain or consequential responses need human review, and academic sources must support the claims made.

Another peer's warning about automation bias highlighted a weakness in relying on human review without specifying how it operates. Lee et al. (2025) associate greater confidence in generative AI with less reported critical thinking, although their survey cannot establish causation. I would therefore give reviewers explicit checking criteria and sufficient time to apply them.

The units also encouraged me to question what evaluation metrics demonstrate. BLEU and ROUGE assess overlap with reference texts, while perplexity reflects predictive performance. None alone establishes factual reliability or creative value. Doshi and Hauser (2024) found improved evaluations but greater similarity among AI-assisted short stories. Their constrained experiment limits generalisation to professional writing. A peer's suggestion to compare collections addresses this collective risk.

My position therefore remains supportive but conditional. I would assess verified quality, total effort and creative diversity, combining relevant metrics with source checks and ongoing monitoring. Independent idea generation and human accountability would remain central to my approach across different practical settings.
